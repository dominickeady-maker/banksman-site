Pricing a building job comes down to seven numbers: labour, materials, plant and hire, subcontractors, overheads, contingency and profit. Add them up in that order, add VAT if you're registered, and you've got a price you can stand behind. Most bad prices aren't bad maths. They're a line that got missed.

Here's a simple method, a worked example, and the free calculators on our [tools page](/tools/) that do the fiddly sums for you.

## 1. Break the job into pieces

Before any numbers, list the work. Strip out, groundworks, brickwork to DPC, up to plate, roof, first fix, plastering, second fix, making good. The smaller the pieces, the less you'll forget.

If you've got drawings, measure from the current revision, not the one on the dashboard of the van. A quote priced off an old drawing is a quote for a different job.

## 2. Labour: days times rates

For each piece of work, ask how many days it will take and who's doing it. Then multiply by their day rate.

If you only know someone's hourly rate, or you need to turn a yearly figure into a day rate, our [day rate calculator](/tools/day-rate-calculator/) converts between hourly, daily, weekly and yearly rates based on the hours and days actually worked.

Be honest about days. Weather, waiting on deliveries and a bit of travel all eat into the week. If you always come in a day over, price the extra day in.

## 3. Materials, with a waste allowance

Price the materials from your merchant's current prices, not last year's. Then add a waste allowance for cuts, breakages and the odd pallet that gets damaged. The right figure depends on the material and the job, so use what your own past jobs tell you.

Don't forget the small stuff: ties, DPC, fixings, sealant, sundries. On their own they're nothing. Across a job they add up.

## 4. Plant, hire and skips

Scaffold, mixers, a digger, a skip or two, welfare units, fencing. Price them for how long you'll actually need them, plus delivery and collection.

## 5. Subcontractors

Get proper quotes from your subbies and drop them in as they are. If you're the main contractor paying subbies under CIS, the deduction doesn't change your price, but it does change what you pay out and when. Our [CIS calculator](/tools/cis-calculator/) shows what comes off a payment at 20%, 30% or gross payment status.

## 6. Overheads

This is the one most small firms underprice. The van, fuel, insurance, your phone, accountant, software and the hours you spend pricing jobs you don't win. None of it belongs to one job, so it has to be spread across all of them. A common way is to add a percentage to your job costs, worked out from what your overheads cost you over a year.

## 7. Contingency and profit

Contingency covers the unknowns: the soft spot in the trench, the rotten joist behind the plasterboard. Profit is what the business earns for the risk of doing the job. They're not the same thing, so keep them as separate lines.

## A worked example

Here's a small job, two bricklayers and a labourer for four days:

| Line | Working | Amount (£) |
|---|---|---|
| Bricklayers | 2 people × 4 days × 220 a day | 1,760.00 |
| Labourer | 4 days × 150 a day | 600.00 |
| Materials | Including a waste allowance | 1,400.00 |
| Skip | One 8 yard skip | 300.00 |
| **Job costs** | | **4,060.00** |
| Overheads | 10% of job costs | 406.00 |
| Contingency | 5% | 223.30 |
| **Total cost** | | **4,689.30** |
| Profit | 15% markup | 703.40 |
| **Price ex VAT** | | **5,392.70** |
| VAT | 20%, if you're registered | 1,078.54 |
| **Price inc. VAT** | | **6,471.24** |

Day rates are what you pay your team; VAT only comes in on the price you charge the customer. The rates and percentages are examples, not recommendations, so use your own.
