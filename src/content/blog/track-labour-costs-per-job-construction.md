> **Quick answer:** To track labour costs per job in construction, link each week's approved timesheets to the site they were worked on and multiply by each worker's hourly rate. Compare the running total with the labour figure in your quote every Friday. That shows you an overrun while the job's still live, instead of finding out from the accountant months later.

Plenty of small building firms price well and still finish the year wondering where the money went. The quote was fine. What went wrong happened after the job started, a few hours at a time. Learning to **track labour costs per job** in construction is how you catch it.

This guide is about what happens once you're on site. If you want help putting the price together in the first place, start with [how to price a building job](/blog/how-to-price-a-building-job/).

## Why do profitable construction quotes end up breaking even?

Usually because labour runs over and nobody sees it until the job's finished. Materials are bought and invoiced, so they're visible, but labour leaks out in small amounts across weeks and sites.

It's the "busy but broke" trap. The bank balance looks healthy because two good jobs are paying on time, and that hides the third job quietly costing more in wages than you'll ever bill for it. By the time the accountant pulls the year together, the money's gone and the lessons are months old.

## What are the biggest sources of labour margin leakage?

On most small jobs it comes down to three things: work that drags on, hours split between sites without being logged properly, and client extras done for free.

1. **Work dragging on.** Groundworks hit a soft spot, the weather turns, a delivery's late, and four days becomes seven. Nobody decides to overspend. It just happens a day at a time.
2. **Split weeks across two jobs.** Someone does Monday on one site and the rest of the week on another, and the timesheet says "5 days, Mill Lane". One job looks cheap, the other looks expensive, and neither is right.
3. **Unbilled client extras.** "While you're here, could you just…" A bit of extra paving, a moved socket, a wider opening. Each one is small, and none of them get billed because nobody wrote them down separately.

Late snagging visits add to it. Going back three times to fix the same thing is labour you priced as zero.

## End-of-quarter spreadsheets vs weekly site-by-site labour tracking

| | End-of-quarter accountant spreadsheets | Weekly site-by-site labour tracking |
|---|---|---|
| **When you spot overruns** | Months after the job's finished | The same week it starts happening |
| **Unbilled extras** | Lost inside the job total | Logged separately, so they can be billed |
| **Admin time** | A big painful catch up every quarter | About ten minutes every Friday |
| **Margin protection** | None: you find out too late | You can act while the job is live |
| **Accuracy of hours** | Rebuilt from memory and bank statements | Recorded per site, the day they're worked |

## How do you calculate live labour spend on a building site?

Multiply each person's approved hours on that site by their hourly rate, and add them up. Do it weekly, not at the end, and keep a running total for each job.

You need three things for each site:

- **Hours per person, per site.** Not "a week at Station Road", but the actual hours on that site, logged on the day.
- **Each person's rate.** Set it once. For employees, use their real cost to you, not just their pay rate, so holiday and employer costs are covered.
- **The labour figure from your quote.** The number of days you priced, times the rates you priced them at.

## How do you track labour spend in ten minutes a week?

Set the rates once, log hours against sites every day, and look at the totals every Friday. The weekly check is the part that matters.

1. **Set every worker's rate once.** Employees and day rate subbies both. Price work is different, because you've agreed a price for the job, so track that separately.
2. **Log hours against the site, every day.** On a phone, at the end of the day, while people remember. This is the step that makes everything else accurate.
3. **Approve the week on Friday.** Check the hours look right before anyone's paid.
4. **Compare spend with the quote.** For each site, put labour spent so far next to the labour you quoted. If you're 70% through the budget and 50% through the work, you know where to look.
5. **Pull out the extras.** Anything the client asked for that wasn't in the quote goes on its own list, ready to bill.

The point isn't perfect accounting. It's seeing on a Friday in March that Station Road has almost used its labour budget with the roof still to do, instead of finding out in June.
